Origin of materials

The oldest material humans ever learned to shape.

Long before anyone quarried a slab for a kitchen island, stone was the material that got humanity through its first tools, its first monuments, and its first permanent buildings. The materials in your home today are the latest chapter of a very long story.

The first material

Before metal, before writing, there was stone.

The earliest stone tools found so far date back well over three million years — older than our own species. For most of human history, stone wasn't a design choice; it was the only durable material available. It shaped the first blades, the first ground-and-polished axes, and eventually the first permanent structures, because unlike wood or hide, worked stone didn't rot, burn, or wear out in a generation.

By the time agriculture and early cities appeared, stone had already made the leap from tool to architecture — and the ambition of what people were willing to move and shape it into kept growing.

Monuments and mountains

Moving mountains, one block at a time.

The pyramids at Giza, Stonehenge, the megaliths scattered across nearly every continent — all built using nothing but manual labour, timber, rope, and an enormous amount of patience, centuries before anyone had powered machinery. Some of those blocks weigh more than a loaded truck, moved and set with a precision that still isn't fully understood.

The Romans industrialised quarrying at a scale the ancient world hadn't seen — the same Carrara quarries that supplied Michelangelo were already being worked in Roman times. Stone stopped being just functional and became a statement: whoever could move the biggest block, cut it the cleanest, and set it the most precisely was making a claim about permanence and power.

From palace to kitchen

The last hundred years changed everything.

For most of that history, working stone at all was slow, expensive, and physically brutal — which is exactly why it was reserved for temples, palaces, and monuments, not homes. Diamond tooling, powered saws, and eventually computer-guided cutting changed that arithmetic completely over the twentieth century, turning a job that once took a quarry crew months into one a small team can finish in days.

Then came a genuinely new idea: instead of only cutting stone, engineer it. Vacuum-compacted quartz surfaces arrived in the 1960s, followed decades later by large-format sintered porcelain slabs — materials that didn't exist as recently as your parents' generation, built specifically to solve the durability and consistency problems natural stone can't. That's the real story of the last hundred years: what used to require a quarry, a fortune, and a palace to own is now something almost any home can have a piece of.

The material families

Nine surface families. Nine very different beginnings.

Natural stone is shaped by geology; engineered, porcelain and terrazzo surfaces are shaped through controlled manufacture. These are the same material families available in the Explore Materials palette.

Metamorphic

Marble

Marble begins as limestone, much of it laid down on ancient seabeds from shells and calcium-rich sediment. When heat and pressure reach it deep underground, the original stone recrystallises and becomes denser, stronger and softly luminous.

The veins are part of that geological story: mineral traces carried through the stone as it folded and changed. Every slab records a different moment of pressure, movement and time.

Typical origins: Italy, Turkey, Greece, Spain and Portugal

Igneous

Granite

Granite forms when molten rock cools very slowly in vast chambers beneath the earth. That unhurried cooling gives quartz, feldspar and mica time to grow into the interlocking crystals visible across its face.

The result is dense, heat-resistant and structurally dependable. Its granular pattern is not printed or layered on; it runs through the full depth of the stone.

Typical origins: South Africa, Zimbabwe, Brazil, India and Norway

Metamorphic

Quartzite

Quartzite starts as sandstone. Tectonic pressure and intense heat force its quartz grains to recrystallise and fuse until the softer sediment becomes one of the hardest natural stones used in interiors.

It can carry the sweeping movement associated with marble, but its quartz structure gives it a very different level of scratch and heat resistance.

Typical origins: Brazil, India, the United States and Europe

Mineral deposit

Onyx

Decorative onyx grows slowly where mineral-rich water leaves thin deposits of calcite in caves and springs. Layer after layer builds its ribbons of cream, amber, green and rust.

Because light can pass through the crystalline bands, onyx behaves less like an ordinary work surface and more like a natural light feature when it is carefully backlit.

Typical origins: Turkey, Iran, Pakistan and Mexico

Sedimentary

Limestone

Limestone is the compressed memory of shallow seas. Shell fragments, marine organisms and calcium-rich sediment settled in layers, then compacted over millions of years into a warm and quietly textured stone.

Fossil traces and tonal shifts often remain visible, giving limestone a softer, calmer character than crystalline granite or highly veined marble.

Typical origins: Portugal, Italy, France, Egypt and Turkey

Explore limestone directions
Sedimentary

Travertine

Travertine forms around hot springs and mineral-rich waterways, where moving water leaves calcium carbonate behind in successive bands. Gas bubbles and organic material create the pores that define its surface.

Cut across those layers it appears cloudy and open; cut with them, it reveals the long linear grain that has made travertine recognisable since Roman architecture.

Typical origins: Turkey, Italy and Iran

Engineered surface

Engineered Stone

Engineered stone turns quartz minerals into a controlled slab. Crushed quartz is blended with pigment and resin, placed under vacuum, then vibrated and compacted until the mix becomes a dense, consistent surface.

The modern process emerged in Italy during the 1960s. It exchanged some of natural stone's unpredictability for repeatable pattern, low porosity and dependable day-to-day performance.

Manufactured globally and supplied through established South African ranges

Sintered ceramic

Large-Format Porcelain

Porcelain begins with finely ground clay, feldspar and silica. The mineral blend is pressed under enormous force and fired close to 1200°C, where the particles sinter together without fully melting.

Porcelain itself is ancient; the enormous slab format is modern. Continuous pressing now creates thin architectural sheets large enough for islands, shower walls and façades with very few joins.

Manufactured globally and specified through South African slab suppliers

Read the porcelain guide
Composite surface

Terrazzo

Terrazzo grew from a practical idea: save fragments of valuable marble, set them into a binder, then grind the surface flat until the stone is revealed again. Venetian craftspeople refined the method centuries ago.

Modern terrazzo uses cementitious or resin binders with stone, marble or glass aggregate. Its colour and scale can be controlled, while the finished surface still carries the irregularity of real material.

Rooted in Italian craft and available from South African manufacturers
